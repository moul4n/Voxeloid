Has Someone Made This

A survey of every game found that shares any part of the concept — accreting matter into a planet, building a star, cross-section worlds, nucleosynthesis as a tech tree, a robot placed by something larger to build worlds. The short version: nothing combines them, one game collides with the pitch, and one twelve-year-old browser toy already proved the element tree works.

Verdict

The combination is original. The parts aren't.

Four things carry the design: a cutaway cross-section as the playfield, a character standing inside the thing being built, matter that physically sprays and settles into layers that seal and merge, and real stellar nucleosynthesis as the progression spine. Plenty of games hit one of those. Nothing found hits two together.

That's the normal shape of an original game, and it's worth being clear-eyed about it: originality here is a combination, not an invention. Every individual idea in the design has been touched by something. What nobody has done is put them in the same game.

One game to go and play before anything else. A Game About Making A Planet came out on Steam in May 2026. Mechanically it is a different thing, but the one-line pitch overlaps enough that press and store algorithms will put you next to it. An evening with it is cheap insurance.

The difference at a glance

What the screen looks like

The clearest way to see the gap is to draw the playfields side by side. Viewpoint is the thing that separates this design from everything near it: five of these six are looking at a world from outside, or at a menu. One is inside.

Read across the row and the pattern is obvious. Everything in the space either floats outside a body looking in, or abandons the playfield entirely for a menu. The cutaway with a character at the centre of it is empty ground, and it's also the thing that will read instantly in a screenshot — which matters more than it sounds, given how crowded the store search is.

Near neighbours

The three closest

A Game About Making A Planet

Acrylic Pixel Games · Steam, 4 May 2026 · about £4 · 49 reviews, 59% positive (Mixed) · 1–2 hours

A 2D minimalist incremental. You merge particles with a gravity-pulsing cursor and merged particles get absorbed into a growing central planet. There's a small skill tree (asteroids, gravity beams) and an endless mode. The prototype is still up on itch.io, where it has 4.2 out of 5 from 29 ratings.

Shares 2D, incremental, gravity, particles accumulating into a planet, planet growth as the progress bar, and — awkwardly — most of the elevator pitch.

Differs Top-down and abstract rather than a cross-section. No player character. No layers, no sealing, no merging. No heat or pressure. No elements or nucleosynthesis. No star. One to two hours against eight to twelve. It's a cursor toy, not an embodied loop.

The mixed reception and tiny scope mean it hasn't claimed the space. But it exists, it's recent, and the overlap is in exactly the part a store algorithm reads. Play it.

Fe[26]

"newbrict" · 2014 · free browser game · no commercial presence

A 2048 variant where you slide and fuse tiles along genuine stellar reaction pathways — hydrogen to helium to carbon and oxygen, on up through neon and magnesium to iron. The game ends at iron, explicitly, because stars cannot fuse past it. Gizmodo covered it and called it shockingly addictive.

Shares The exact scientific progression, including the iron wall used as a designed ceiling rather than a piece of trivia.

Differs A ten-minute puzzle game. No accretion, nothing spatial, no planet, no character, no economy.

This is the encouraging find. Somebody worked out twelve years ago that the fusion chain makes a good ruleset, and then nobody attached it to a building game. The idea is proven and unoccupied at the same time.

Solar 2

Jay Watts / Murudai · 2011 · PC, Xbox 360, Mac, iOS, Android · Metacritic 72 (8 reviews) · won Microsoft Dream Build Play 2011

The canonical accrete-mass-and-climb-the-ladder game. You start as an asteroid, absorb mass, and become a planet, then a life-bearing planet, then a small star, a large star, a neutron star, a black hole, and finally the Big Crunch and a new Big Bang. Its 2009 predecessor Solar sold around thirty thousand copies, enough to fund the sequel full-time.

Shares Mass accretion as the spine, the star-then-planets cosmology, and the emotional arc of becoming something enormous.

Differs Top-down open space. You are the body rather than a machine inside it. No interior, no layers, no cross-section, no elements, no refining, no incremental systems. An arcade sandbox, not a builder.

Fifteen years old and structurally different, but it's the obvious comparison for anyone who covers the game. Easier to name it as an influence than to have it pointed out.

A machine placed by a creator to build worlds

Nothing matching. The nearest are Terraforming Earth (Andras Csibi, Steam — robots rebuilding a dead Earth across generations, 2D side view, but a platformer on an existing planet) and Teo: The Last Terraformer (a jam-scale itch.io game about a small terraforming robot). The specific framing — a small machine at the core of a world it is building, put there by something larger that never explains itself — is unoccupied, and it's probably the strongest identity hook in the whole design.

Real nucleosynthesis as progression

Only Fe[26], above. Universe Sandbox models accretion physically but is a simulator rather than a game with progression. Falling-sand games like Sandboxels have element reaction tables but no gravitational planet formation and no tech tree. Nothing found uses the hydrogen-to-iron climb, the iron wall, and the supernova that makes everything heavier as a game-long unlock spine.

Conclusion

What's actually unclaimed

  • The cutaway cross-section as the playfield. Everything nearby looks at a world from outside or replaces the world with a menu.
  • A character embodied inside the thing being built, at the centre, never leaving until the work is done.
  • Matter that physically sprays and settles into persistent layers which then seal and merge, rather than a number going up.
  • Heat and pressure emerging from your own accumulated mass, flipping from goal in act one to hazard in act two.
  • Nucleosynthesis as the unlock tree, with the iron wall as the act break.
  • Star first, then planets from its debris, with per-world resets and knowledge as the only thing that travels.

What to lead with. The element tree is the sharpest differentiator, because it's real, it's legible in a sentence, and exactly one twelve-year-old browser game has ever used it. The cross-section is the sharpest visual differentiator, because it makes a screenshot that nothing else in the category can make. Those two together are the pitch.

Risks

Hazards worth naming now

The pitch collision. A Game About Making A Planet occupies the same sentence. It's small and mixed, so it hasn't claimed the space, but the comparison will be made. The defence is that the screenshots look nothing alike, which is another reason the cross-section earns its place.

The crowded search space. There are at least eight games with near-identical "idle planet" names, all menu clickers. Anything with "planet" and "idle" in the title disappears into them. The title and the first screenshot have to do the work of signalling that this isn't one of those. "Voxeloid" is distinctive enough; the risk is in the subtitle and the tags.

The Solar 2 comparison. Inevitable in any coverage. Get ahead of it by naming it as an influence rather than waiting to be told.

The unreleased one. Cosmic Accretion is in active development in Godot and shares the strike-matter-to-grow-a-body idea. It's top-down with no interior, so not a direct threat, but it's the only thing found that's currently being built in the same neighbourhood.
