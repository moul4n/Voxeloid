Particles Without Collisions

The CPU is the bottleneck because the simulation is asking the wrong question. Hundreds of thousands of particles is not a collision problem to be optimised — it is a collision problem to be deleted. Here is how the games that do this actually do it, a working prototype that holds 100,000 particles at 8 ms in JavaScript, and what it means for the Godot build.

Diagnosis

Why the CPU is stacking up

If every particle is an object with a collider, the engine has to answer "what is this touching?" for every one of them, every frame. Even with a good broad phase that is a spatial query per particle, plus the bookkeeping of keeping a live handle, a transform, and a physics body in sync. At a few thousand it is fine. At a hundred thousand it is hopeless, and no amount of optimising the query will save it, because the query shouldn't exist.

The games that do this at scale — Noita, the falling-sand lineage, and by all appearances Gnorp — are not running faster collision. They have no collision system at all for this material. That is the whole trick, and everything below follows from it.

The idea

Particles collide with a field, never with each other

Keep one shared structure that describes where the world's surface is. A particle in flight asks that structure a single question each frame — am I below the surface at my angle? — which is one array read at a computed index. If the answer is yes, the particle stops existing and its mass is added to the field.

Two things fall out of that immediately. The cost per particle is constant and tiny, so the total is linear in the number of particles in flight. And the number in flight is bounded by how much is currently in the air, not by how much material the planet contains — because the moment material settles it stops being a particle and becomes a number in an array.

The falling-sand engines say the same thing in a different shape: in a grid, a cell's neighbours are array indices. The cell below index i is i + width. There is nothing to search for, so there is no broad phase, no spatial hash, no collision pairs, and the inner loop walks memory in a straight line.

Architecture

Three representations, and material moves between them

Nothing in the game is "a particle" for its whole life. Material passes through three states, each with a cost profile that suits what it is doing at the time.

The field

A radial height field: for each of 720 angular columns, the radius of the surface, plus a coarse stack of material bands below it. That is 720 floats and about 69 KB of material IDs, total, for a whole planet. It does not grow. Merging deep layers, which the design already calls for, is an operation on this array and nothing else.

Flight

Packed parallel arrays — x, y, vx, vy, material — with a live count and swap-removal, so dead slots cost nothing. Motion is ballistic under gravity pointing at the centre. The only test is against the field. This is exactly the structure the voxbench swarm already has; it needs the gravity direction changed and the landing test added.

Surface flow

One pass along the columns: where the height difference between neighbours exceeds what the material can hold, move the excess. Ten passes over 720 columns measured at 0.15 ms and forty passes at 0.36 ms, regardless of how much material is on the planet. This is what makes sand pile into slopes and water level out, and it is the cheapest part of the whole system.

Materials

Two numbers give you water through solid rock

The angle of repose is the steepest slope a granular material holds before it slides. It is a real, tabulated physical property, and it is the entire difference between sand and water in this model.

MaterialAngle of reposeBehaves as
Water0°Levels out completely, finds the lowest point
Water-filled sand15–30°Slumps and spreads, holds a shallow bank
Dry sand34°The classic cone
Granite (loose)35–40°Coarse scree slope
Snow38°Holds, then slides in sheets
Crushed stone, gravel45°Steep, chunky, stable
Solid rockeffectively 90°Doesn't flow at all — vertical faces hold

The second number is flow rate: how much of the excess moves per pass. High flow plus zero angle gives you water that sloshes level in a moment. Low flow plus a steep angle gives you tar that creeps. Between them sit every powder, gravel and slurry you need, and the elements from the design's tree each get their own pair.

This is the same solver for everything. One height field, two parameters. Real-time height-field sand and water research (Su et al., SIGGRAPH Asia 2023) runs 512×1024 to 1024×1024 grids at 0.46–3.54 ms per step, and explicitly compares against particle methods: full 3D material-point simulation of the same scene costs around 700 ms per step. Roughly two to three orders of magnitude, for behaviour that reads the same on screen.

Impacts

Meteors: field to flight, on a budget

An impact is the transition running backwards. Read the field over the impact arc, remove material to carve the crater, and convert that mass into flight particles thrown outward.

The important detail is that the mass removed and the particles spawned are separate numbers. If an impact displaces enough material to justify fifty thousand particles, spawn five thousand and move the rest of the mass directly in the field. The crater is correct, the debris arc looks right, and nobody counts the grains. The budget is a hard cap on the spawn, and when the flight pool is full you settle the oldest particles rather than refusing to spawn — so a big impact never blows the frame.

The same budget covers the design's "meteors throw up all the loose particles" moment. Loose surface material within the blast radius is converted to flight up to the cap; beyond the cap it is simply redistributed in the field, which the surface flow pass then smooths out over the next second or two.

Scaling

Three tiers, each cheaper than the last

The prototype is tier one. If it is not enough, there are two more steps, and the third is the one that actually ends the problem.

TierWhat the CPU does per particle per frameCeiling
1 · CPU flightIntegrate, one field lookup, maybe depositMeasured 81 ns in JS. GDScript will be several times slower, so realistically tens of thousands.
2 · Shader flightNothing. The vertex shader integrates from spawn data; the CPU only writes a slot on spawn.Hundreds of thousands. This is what voxbench already does — it just needs radial gravity.
3 · Cohort schedulingNothing, and it doesn't even track them.Effectively unbounded.

Why tier three ends it

Ballistic motion is deterministic. A particle's entire future is fixed the moment it is launched. So the CPU does not need to watch it fly — it can work out at spawn time roughly when and where it will land, and put an entry in a list of pending deposits. Every frame it pops the deposits that are due. The particle itself is drawn by the GPU and never touched again.

Better still, particles launched in the same frame with similar parameters land at similar times. So schedule cohorts, not particles: one trajectory solve per cohort, one deposit event that spreads mass across a spread of columns. At a couple of hundred cohorts a second the CPU cost of the entire material system rounds to zero, whether the GPU is drawing ten thousand particles or a million.

The cost is a small loss of fidelity: the cohort deposits as a distribution rather than as individual grains landing. In a cross-section where each grain is a pixel or two, that is not a visible difference. It also breaks if a particle's path can change mid-flight — so anything that needs to be deflected, blown about or caught by a moving arm stays in tier two, which is fine, because that is a small minority.

The per-particle cost is flat across two orders of magnitude, which is the point: the system is genuinely linear, with no hidden term that bites at scale. And the surface flow pass, which is doing the actual material behaviour, costs 0.15 ms at ten passes and 0.36 ms at forty — independent of both particle count and how much material the planet holds.

Read this honestly. Those are JavaScript numbers. GDScript's interpreter is slower per element even over packed arrays — community measurements put typed GDScript at roughly 1.6× faster than untyped, and GDScript in general well behind C# and far behind native, though I could not find an authoritative current figure. Assume the CPU path in GDScript caps out somewhere in the tens of thousands, and plan on tier two or three rather than hoping.

Important

Godot reality check, and a decision to revisit

The Compatibility renderer does not support compute shaders, RenderingDevice access, Texture2DRD, GPU particle SDF collision, particle trails, or emit_particle(). All of these are listed as "Not supported" for Compatibility in Godot's official renderer comparison. We picked Compatibility for voxbench on day one for the low-spec floor. That choice is now load-bearing and needs a deliberate second look.

The good news is that this architecture does not need any of them, and that is not a coincidence — it is a consequence of using a height field rather than a full cellular grid. A falling-sand simulation over a million cells genuinely wants compute. A 720-column height field is small enough to live on the CPU and be uploaded as a tiny texture.

CapabilityCompatibilityDo we need it?
Compute shadersNoNo — the field is 720 columns, not a million cells
Particle SDF collisionNoNo — we collide against our own field, which is cheaper and gives us the deposit event
Vertex-shader particle motionYesYes, and this is tier two of the ladder
MultiMesh with custom dataYesYes — already working in voxbench
SubViewport ping-pongYesOnly if we ever want the field on the GPU; RGBA8 and one frame of latency
emit_particle()NoNo — we spawn through MultiMesh slots ourselves

Two things to verify on the dev PC before committing

ImageTexture.update cost. There is a reported regression (Godot issue #76994) where a 1920×1080 ImageTexture::update() took 32–38 ms in Godot 4.0.2 against under 1 ms in 3.5. If that is still true in 4.4, any per-frame CPU-to-GPU image upload is dead. Our field texture would be tiny — 720×96 — so it is probably fine either way, but measure it rather than assume. There is also no partial-region update API, so you always upload the whole texture.

MultiMesh buffer writes. Godot's own optimisation docs recommend RenderingServer.multimesh_set_buffer() with one linear PackedFloat32Array over per-instance setters, explicitly for cache efficiency, and say that for thousands of constantly-updated instances going through the servers directly is the recommended approach. I found no official quantified speedup, so measure it — but it is also the plan-B already written into the phase 0 build plan for the spike scenario.

One open issue worth knowing about: there is currently no way to have a compute shader write a MultiMesh buffer that stays GPU-resident without a CPU round trip (Godot issue #105113), and no partial buffer updates (proposal #957). Both constrain the clean version of tier two on the Vulkan renderers.

Research

What the games that do this actually do

Noita — the reference implementation

From Petri Purho's GDC 2019 talk. The world is a grid of material IDs in 64×64 chunks. Each chunk keeps a dirty rect — the bounding box of what changed last frame — and a chunk with an empty dirty rect is skipped entirely. That is sleeping, expressed spatially. Threading is a four-pass checkerboard: each pass updates a set of non-adjacent chunks, and because a pixel can never move more than half a chunk in one pass, no two threads can touch the same region. No locks, no atomics.

Two details worth stealing outright. The update is single-buffered and runs bottom-to-top in place, which is why sand piles instead of smearing. And when a chunk of terrain is blasted free, Noita runs marching squares over the outline and hands the polygon to Box2D as a rigid body — a two-way conversion between field and object, which is the same pattern as our flight-and-deposit.

Nolla have never published a pixels-per-second figure, and the acknowledged failure mode is honest: when everything is moving at once, dirty rects stop helping and the frame rate goes with it.

If we ever do want a real cellular grid

Running falling-sand on the GPU hits one hard problem: two cells can both want to move into the same cell, and in a parallel write one silently wins, so mass is created or destroyed. The standard fix is the Margolus neighbourhood — partition into disjoint 2×2 blocks, let one thread decide all four cells together, and shift the partition origin on alternate steps so material can cross boundaries. It works, and its known artefact is that sand falls with visible gaps and a checkerboard rhythm because a grain can only move once per block phase. The cleaner alternative is a "pull" formulation where each cell asks what would move into it, so every thread writes only its own cell — structurally conflict-free, but awkward to express for liquids.

Worth knowing, not worth building. Our material is radially stratified around a centre, which a height field describes almost perfectly and a square grid describes badly.

Actions

What to change in voxbench

  1. Swap the gravity direction in swarm.gdshader — acceleration toward a centre point instead of a constant downward vector. Already on the list from the design doc; it is a couple of lines.
  2. Add the field. A PackedFloat32Array of 720 column heights and a PackedByteArray of material bands, plus the deposit function. This is the new core.
  3. Add the landing test. On the CPU path it is the one array read shown above. On the GPU path the shader kills the particle visually at the surface and the CPU learns about the deposit through whichever tier you are on.
  4. Add the relaxation pass with per-material angle and flow. This is the cheapest thing in the whole system and the one that makes it look like a planet rather than a pile of dots.
  5. Add a "material" scenario to scenarios.json — sustained deposit rate, a scripted meteor at a fixed time, and the field at late-game size — so the benchmark measures what the game will actually do rather than a debris shower.
  6. Re-run the renderer shoot-out with this in mind. Compatibility is still viable for this design, but the capability table above is the thing to weigh, and it is worth knowing what Forward+ buys before the project is too deep to move.
  7. Measure ImageTexture.update() on 4.4 before relying on any CPU-to-GPU field upload.

The thing to hold on to. The number of particles you can afford is not a property of your collision code. It is a property of how long a particle stays a particle. Make landing cheap and immediate, and the count takes care of itself.
